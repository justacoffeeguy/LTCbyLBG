A Reality Most Families Underestimate

One of Retirement's Biggest Blind Spots

Long-term care is one of the biggest expenses most families never see coming - and it can put retirement savings at risk.

What Long-Term Care Actually Means

Long-term care is the ongoing help someone needs with everyday activities - bathing, dressing, moving around, managing medications - when age, illness, or injury make it hard to manage alone. It can start small, with a little help at home, and build gradually from there, or arrive all at once after a fall or diagnosis. Either way, it's different from the short-term, acute medical care most health insurance is actually built around.

Roughly 70% of adults who reach age 65 will need some form of long-term care in their remaining lifetime. Nearly half of those older adults - about 48% to 51% - will require paid care, while others rely on unpaid help from family and friends.

Sources: HHS/ASPE, "What Is the Lifetime Risk of Needing and Receiving Long-Term Services and Supports?"; Administration for Community Living, acl.gov/ltc; SingleCare, "Long-Term Care Statistics"; AARP LTSS Choices
The Numbers, Plainly

Private insurance generally doesn't cover this. Medicare's coverage is limited to specific circumstances.

$120,900
Average future long-term care cost for an American turning 65 today. Families pay more than a third of that directly out of pocket.
Source: HHS/ASPE, "LTSS: Risks and Financing," 2022, cited in KFF, 2024
$62,400–$288,288
2023 median annual cost range - from full-time homemaker help to round-the-clock home health aide care. A private nursing home room averaged $116,800.
Source: KFF, "10 Things About Long-Term Services and Supports," 2024
$36,000 / $103,800
Median annual income and median total savings for Medicare beneficiaries in 2023 - both far below what a single year of full-time care costs.
Source: KFF, "Income and Assets of Medicare Beneficiaries in 2023"
What Medicare Actually Covers

Medicare covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing care after a qualifying hospital stay - not ongoing custodial care at home or in a facility.

Private health insurance generally excludes long-term services and supports (LTSS) entirely. In one national survey, 4 in 10 adults incorrectly believed Medicare was the primary way people pay for long-term nursing or home care over an extended period.

Source: Medicare.gov coverage guidelines; KFF Health Costs polling, cited in "10 Things About Long-Term Services and Supports," 2024

How do I protect myself and my loved ones?

Planning for long-term care starts with understanding your options. While there are several ways to pay for care, each comes with its own benefits, limitations, and tradeoffs. Knowing the difference can help you make decisions with confidence.
