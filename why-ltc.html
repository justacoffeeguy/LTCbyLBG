Understanding Your Options

Why Long-Term Care Insurance Matters

Most families assume Medicare, Medicaid, or their own savings will cover it if the time comes. Here's where each one actually falls short.

Understand the risk

Long-term care can affect far more than the person receiving it. Before looking at how to pay for care, it helps to understand what creates the need - and who carries the burden when there isn't a plan.

How Care Begins

Who actually needs long-term care?

Long-term care isn't limited to one diagnosis or one age. The need usually begins when a person can no longer safely manage everyday activities on their own, or when cognitive impairment makes ongoing supervision necessary.

Aging & physical decline

As strength and mobility decline, everyday tasks like bathing, dressing, and moving safely can become difficult without help.

Debilitating injury

A fall or accident can create months of rehabilitation and hands-on help, even for someone who was fully independent before.

Progressive illness

Conditions such as stroke or Parkinson's can reduce motor function and create a need for sustained assistance over years, not weeks.

Cognitive impairment

Dementia and related conditions can create a need for supervision because of memory loss, poor judgment, or disorientation - even without a physical limitation.

Multiple chronic conditions

Often there is no single event. A gradual accumulation of health conditions can eventually make regular, ongoing support necessary.

Medicare

Built for short-term needs, not ongoing care.

Medicare is the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older. It's excellent at what it's designed for - hospital stays, physician visits, prescription drugs, short-term rehabilitation. It was never built to fund years of custodial help with daily living.

What it actually covers

Up to 100 days of skilled nursing care per benefit period, but only after a qualifying 3-day inpatient hospital stay. The first 20 days are covered in full; days 21-100 require a daily co-payment.

Home care is narrowly limited

Medicare will cover home health services for someone who is homebound and needs skilled care, generally up to about 28 hours a week - not full-time, and not for someone who simply needs help with daily activities.

What it does not cover

Long-term custodial care - help with bathing, dressing, toileting, or supervision for dementia - is explicitly excluded. Once Medicare's short-term coverage ends, any Medicare supplement policy stops paying too.

The knowledge gap

In one national survey, 4 in 10 adults incorrectly believed Medicare was the primary way people pay for long-term nursing or home care over an extended period. It isn't.

Sources: Medicare.gov, "Skilled Nursing Facility (SNF) Care" and "Home Health Services"; KFF, "10 Things About Long-Term Services and Supports," 2024
Medicaid

Coverage exists - but only after you've spent down what you have.

Medicaid is a jointly funded federal-state program, and it does cover long-term custodial care - unlike Medicare. But qualifying requires becoming financially depleted first, and even then, access and choice of care are far more limited than most people expect.

The asset test

To qualify, an individual generally can't have more than about $2,000 in countable savings and investments beyond a primary residence, one car, and a small burial or life insurance policy. Rules vary by state and marital status.

Where the money actually goes

Medicaid paid for more than half of the $415 billion the U.S. spent on long-term services and supports in 2022 - but most of that pays for home and community-based services, not the nursing facility care people often assume.

Waiting lists are real

Over 692,000 people nationally are on waiting lists or interest lists for Medicaid home and community-based services - qualifying financially doesn't always mean immediate access to care.

Choice narrows considerably

Many facilities are private-pay only, and those that do accept Medicaid often have a limited number of Medicaid-designated beds - meaning where you receive care may not be up to you.

Sources: Les Goldstein, client planning materials, 2026; KFF, "10 Things About Long-Term Services and Supports," 2024; KFF, "A Look at Waiting Lists for Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services From 2016 to 2023"

What happens when out-of-pocket care lasts longer than expected

Self-insuring means using your own savings and income to cover care as it's needed. It offers full control and no premiums - until care lasts longer than expected and the cost keeps building year after year.

A Real Client Example

One client met Les later in life, around age 60. By then, changes in her health meant she was no longer able to qualify for long-term care insurance. She ultimately needed care for eleven years. Between 2007 and 2018, she went through more than $1.3 million of her own savings - everything she had, except her condominium. She had planned carefully in every other way. Long-term care was the one piece she had not been able to put in place.
